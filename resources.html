About MP3.com

Context for why this archive matters — the platform WoMP3 lived on, and the legal battles that shaped digital music history.

Origins and Rise · 1997–1999

An Independent Music Revolution

MP3.com was launched in 1997 by entrepreneur Michael Robertson. Originally, it began as a simple resource and informational site tracking MP3 technology, encoders, and early hardware players. The site quickly pivoted into a massive web hosting and distribution platform for independent and unsigned artists. Long before Myspace, SoundCloud, or Spotify, MP3.com allowed anyone to upload their music for free.

Artists received artist pages, detailed download statistics, and a global audience. For music fans, it was a goldmine of legal, free, downloadable MP3 tracks across every imaginable niche genre, making it one of the most heavily trafficked sites on the web. In July 1999, MP3.com went public with one of the most explosive IPOs of the dot-com era, briefly driving the company's market value into the billions.

The Landmark Legal Battle · 2000

UMG v. MP3.com

While indie artists loved MP3.com, major record labels viewed the platform with extreme hostility. The friction peaked with the launch of "My.MP3.com" in early 2000 — a service that allowed users to listen to CDs they owned from any computer, by verifying ownership and streaming from MP3.com's central servers.

Major record labels (led by Universal Music Group) sued MP3.com for massive copyright infringement, arguing that MP3.com had illegally built a centralized database containing thousands of commercially released albums without authorization. The court ruled severely against MP3.com, calling its actions "wholesale copying of copyrighted material to make commercial profits." Judge Jed Rakoff imposed staggering statutory damages — totaling $53.4 million to Universal alone, with pending settlements from other major labels. This crippling legal liability forced the company to settle out of court, severely wounding its finances.

Acquisition and Decline · 2001–2003

Vivendi, CNET, and Shutdown

To survive the crushing debt from the lawsuits, MP3.com was acquired in August 2001 by media conglomerate Vivendi Universal — which conveniently owned the label that had sued them. Under Vivendi, the site was heavily corporate-vetted and lost much of its rebellious, grass-roots independent identity.

As the dot-com bubble burst and Vivendi downsized, CNET Networks bought the MP3.com domain name and select assets in 2003. CNET officially shut down the original music-sharing service on December 2, 2003, transforming the domain into an editorial tech-news and music-review blog.

Legacy

What MP3.com Made Possible

  • It proved that consumers hungered for digital, downloadable music delivery long before Apple launched the iTunes Store.
  • It paved the way for democratized music distribution, showing that independent artists could bypass traditional record label gatekeepers to reach a global fanbase.
  • Alongside peer-to-peer services like Napster, MP3.com sat at ground zero of the legal and cultural wars that forced the multi-billion-dollar music industry to drag itself into the digital age.
  • Channels like Women of MP3.com demonstrated that niche editorial programming could build dedicated communities on the platform — well before the era of social media influencers or genre-specific streaming playlists.
