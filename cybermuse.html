About CyberMuse / i-Muse

Women of MP3.com was built on many voices — a collective of women, gender-spectrum folx, men, and allies who contributed editorial across the life of the channel. CyberMuse was one of those contributions: a music review column written by editorial contributor Jianda, spotlighting independent artists on the MP3.com platform — reviewing their albums, surfacing their sonic worlds, and connecting them to listeners who might otherwise never have found them.

As the channel evolved, the column's name shifted from CyberMuse to i-Muse. The voice — sharp, warm, deeply curious about sound — remained consistent throughout. Jianda covered gospel, jazz, pop, ambient, electronic, and everything in between, always with ears open to what an artist was actually doing, not just what genre said they should be.

The Fireants
"12 Years"
Jan 19, 2000 Punk / Post-Punk / Spoken Word

Among WO'MP3's digital divas and sonic goddesses, we find Skie Bender — estrogenic cornerstone of The Fireants. What can we make of her? She's either all woman (road-weary poetess), or all madwoman (with Jello Biafra on the side). "12 Years," the droning, twisted tale uploaded on our illustrious web site, might give us some insight. She sings "Your fetish is the FBI / Surveillance cameras in your eyes / catch you dusting fingerprints / can't you see I'm dropping hints?" and we get it: Our nightmare is her daydream.

The push-pull anti-melodies in Bender's voice waft over Kevin Jacobs' and Kelly Busby's guitar-drum "testosteronia" well. The Fireants are too loud for coffee shops, too revelatory for dive bars. Though they're not uploaded, the catchy, spaced-out Powerpop tune "Sun Go Down" and "Congratulations," a song about one woman's psychodrama, are worth shelling out the extra few bones for. Deep thinkers and performance art devotees will love this band.

What's your coping mechanism of choice? It couldn't be music, could it? Inquiring Jianda wants to know.

Umbrella
"Bored"
Jan 26, 2000 Jazz / Blues / Ensemble

The confident strength of Naomi Kukoff's bluesy/jazzy voice alluringly guides you into Umbrella's world. Automatically, you feel cozy there. Umbrella's still a new ensemble though, and it shows in a couple of yin-yang areas. Musically, they're strong songwriters and you get the feeling they love to jam with each other (yang, all the way). The one yin side of the band has to do with momentum.

"Bored" for instance, with its strolling Rickie Lee Jones-ish arrangement, breaks into a panoramic (albeit briefly so) chorus that really drives home the point of the song — it works best here. However, each song's sonic dynamics often creep from a peaceful Sunday-afternoon vibe to a big-production Saturday-night-gig vibe, within measures and without a buildup. These seasoned musicians' diversity of influences mesh well together, which is a rare find. It just seems like they're still riding the waves, right on the crest of an ideal balance.

Xandra
"Leave Him To Me"
Feb 2, 2000 Soft Pop / Harmonics

Xandra sparks memories of 1970s and 80s Soft Pop, best exemplified on her Stacy Lattisaw cover, "Let Me Be Your Angel." Compilations like "Spirit of 73" validate that women's music was just as heartfelt then as it is now. Music makers like Carole King, Ambrosia and Olivia Newton John exuded lush, unabashedly sweet fireside pop vocal stylings. Often more important than the lyrics themselves, soft pop and rock then was all about the production. Atmosphere.

Here — without being hackneyed or trite — Xandra's layered harmonies are transcendent, radio-friendly and warm. Today's pop milieu pretends too often to be "cool" and trendy, while this artist adds a modern spin, even to these songs that reminisce. I so enjoy (and miss) this genuine sound. You GO, Xandra.

Melinda Klump
"The Short Story"
Feb 9, 2000 Singer-Songwriter / Brooding Folk

Melancholy, brooding, droning... these words waft up while you're digging Melinda Klump's voice, chanting lightly over lyrics like "we walk down the street / two blocks seem like twenty" (from "All Over Again"). Klump cites Jeff Buckley as one of her influences — not surprising. The dark cello, too, moans like a drowning maiden, adding more dissonant color to her vignettes.

Portishead, while 180 degrees opposite of Klump, elicits similar emotions: You want to save the character, the anti-heroine brought to life, but you know she has to — and doesn't want to — save herself. She's married to sadness. Even as she sings "hello laughter / hello light filling the sky" (from "Goodbye"), you're not completely convinced.

In her imaginary landscapes, maybe once there was a Shangri-La, but it seems she's lost her way back. She's mourning. She's asking for your help, or maybe she's just asking you to listen. Klump consumes, captivates and puts words to your moodiness.

Denise Tichenor-Collins
"REVELATIONS"
c. 2000 Gospel / R&B / Pop

With a modern verve, Denise Tichenor-Collins takes an old-time Gospel flava and mixes in smooth, well-produced melodies and R&B arrangements. A walk through her release leads us first to "Jazzlude," a slick voice-as-instrument jam that's nearly 100% classic scat. "Near the Cross," aside from its testimony of pure faith, is as slick and soulful as any TLC cut, while the Gospel-Funk song "There's no Me" would fit right in alongside any Winans family album.

Tichenor-Collins displays both her talent, and her loving spirit in this release, which, like Bebe and Cece Winans' tunes, would fare just as well on Top 40 stations as it would on Christian radio. If Soul or Gospel strikes your fancy, this indie EP is a keeper!

Amanda Emery
"Amanda Emery"
c. 2000 Pop / Jazz / Quiet Storm

Emergy. Emergy. As I listened to this album-length body of work over and over, that non-word kept asserting itself: EMERGY. Maybe it's because of this collection's energy: Amanda Emery, powered by influences like Kenny Loggins, comes off cool, mixing pop and jazz (see: Loggins' "This is It") — the "quiet storm" sound. Emergy? Could have to do with "merging." Amanda's hubby Jim backs her up with an impressive array of instrumentation, and the musical progressions are constantly moving, playing with melody and rhythm without being too flashy.

The fact that they're in a relationship, merged, adds a nice color to the romantic flavor of the lyrics and the passion in Emery's voice. "Emergy," too, might mean "emerges." "Wild Ride" and "It Takes You Back" are the standout cuts here.

Amanda and Jim, your "emergy" must make fellow Canadian indie music peers mighty proud. Jianda will show you her Kenny Loggins if you'll show her yours.

Purple Planet
"Purple Planet"
c. 2000–2001 World / Drum 'n' Bass / Electronic

Purple Planet's mix of acoustic and electronic elements breaks down nicely as you take in their highly palatable good trips. Transcending their cross between world music, drum n' bass and alternavibes like that of Garbage and Portishead, this duo creates a refreshing kind of magic, and lyrics like "You're like the first puff on my last cigarette" ("Addicted") and "Nothing could be sweeter than my auto-written destiny" ("Rearview Mirror") feed your head while you're in their dream state.

In David and Cassandra's interplanetary songs of bliss, sweet, charming sentiments reach haunting, edgy heights. Old vinyl samples mix with techno licks and effect-free acoustic guitars. Meanwhile, Cassandra sings sweet, twisted melodies. Bravo for experiential tunes for the cyber-tribalist within. (My fave? "Calamity Jane" captures all these ingredients to a T.)

Basque
"Basque" / "Radiate" / "Falling Forward"
Jan 31, 2001 Ambient / Ethereal / Post-Classical

Basque is your lunar spaceship of sound, taking off and landing in sonic swirls and afterthoughts, waves upon waves, notes upon notes. They're kind of like Enigma without so much percussion, loops or heavy production. Elegant and stately, Maryasque (songstress) and Brandt (bass alchemist) create music that takes its own time leisurely, that's as natural as breathing.

In their own words, "The aim of Basque is to give silence its place again in music. Create space and let people's minds breathe." Hence, I bestow upon you my heartfelt recommendation that you groove on all three of their wondrous, melodious CDs. "In a world filled with opinion and visual statements," the band states, "we want to let the music speak for itself. To evoke emotion from the heart instead of the head. To create (dare we say it) something of beauty that inspires something in others."

Not unlike Siouxsie Sioux or Liz Frazer, Maryasque enunciates every syllable of every word in a luxurious, glorious way. She speaks a language of her own, using her voice as an instrument while Brandt's bass is like a subtly haunting vocalist. If you are into the Cocteau Twins, Dido or MP3.com's Should, you will enjoy Basque. Extra-neato trax include: "swollen stranger" and "Orange Colored Sky."

Pamela Zero
MP3s (a cappella)
Jan 5, 2000 A Cappella / Ethereal / Experimental

That's it — I've been bewitched. As Pamela Zero sings, "Ghosts have somehow moved into my days." I downloaded her MP3s, two at a time. "Entrance" beckoned (think: toning in caves) and "The Yearning" begged me to stay (see: Kate Bush, "Breathing"). Audio surfaced, first in 1 track, then 2, and I let them play simultaneously. "Physics" came in — the last track in this Jungian round — and it felt like cloud-busting.

In Zero's tunes you'll find snippets of Miranda Sex Garden and Dead Can Dance. Did I mention her work is 99.9% a cappella? The arrangements and vocals are so lush, you forget — her larynx IS the instrument. Listening to Zero is like eavesdropping on someone's psyche. Catharsis follows, pure as church bells. Lest you think there's no music in the lot, the blues tune "The Strip Song" carries with it a nice, lively piano accompaniment. Otherwise, it's unfettered coloratura and bass vox, Baby. Enjoy.

Leni Stern
"Finally the Rain has Come" — Feature Article
Jun 10, 2002 Jazz / World / Guitar

Pop Quiz: What HASN'T Leni Stern done? Like a sunrise, Leni's new release "Finally the Rain has Come" warms the heart and penetrates the soul with light. On the eve of Leni's arrival at the MP3.com studios (to promote this album), it was sunny out — not even a sign of dew. Clouds. Anything. The day after her interview, it had rained after many months of San Diego Sunshine.

Such is the magic, and majesty, enveloped in Leni's work, and in this exclusive interview, in which Leni touches on the recording process, how to be resilient as a survivor, how to take care of yourself above all else while remaining compassionate, and how to, namely, "Love Everyone" — one of the many enchanting singles from "Finally..."

If you enjoy Sting, Joni Mitchell, David Byrne, Jane Siberry (we could go on and on, but truly Leni is her own "good trip!") you'll very much enjoy Leni's latest gem. "Finally the Rain has Come" blends sounds of healing, harmony and strength in polyrhythms, skipping through time zones — from India, to Africa, to the streets of NYC.

German-born Leni Stern gave up a successful acting career to study Film Scoring at Berklee, studying privately with guitarist Bill Frisell and pianist/composer Charlie Banacos. She and her husband, guitarist Mike Stern, moved to New York, and they both enjoy successful recording careers. Leni has 11 albums in tow, and has received Gibson Guitar's Best Female Jazz Guitarist award for the past five years in a row. This bright pixie-sprite-woman-girl of the world is a true delight, inspiration, and modern-day heroine.

Lovespirals (Anji)
Interview — "Windblown Kiss" era
Jul 9, 2002 Dream Pop / Ambient / Drum 'n' Bass

Jianda interviewed Anji of Lovespirals for a feature article on the Women of MP3.com Station. Lovespirals emerged from Ryan Lum's earlier project Love Spirals Downwards; Anji joined in early 1999. Their debut album "Windblown Kiss" came out June 18, 2002 on Projekt Records. The interview, preserved on lovespirals.com, is one of the few surviving records of WoMP3's journalism work.

JIANDA: How did you get into music, how long have you been making it, and when did you join Lovespirals?

ANJI: I'd say that I first got into music through my dad. One of my earliest memories is circling around the coffee table to "Here Comes the Sun," when I was barely able to walk. I started singing very early, doing school productions from Pre-School on. Shortly out of High School, I got involved with different garage bands, doing gigs, and recording 4 track demos... Speaking of Lovespirals, I began working with Ryan in early 1999.

JIANDA: Do you have a songwriting routine/regimen, or do you just compose when "the spirit moves you?"

ANJI: Generally, a song lyric and melody will come to me out of the blue, which I'll capture on a little hand held tape recorder. I also write lyrics in my journal, with or without an attendant melody line. For Lovespirals, what generally happens is that Ryan will be messing around — either practicing guitar, or checking out some loops — and something will catch my attention. Oftentimes I'll think, "Hey, that reminds me of that one song idea I was working on!" so I'll go grab my journal and start singing along.

JIANDA: What kinds of feedback do you get from your fans? Your music has a very gentle, healing quality to it; do you get that mirrored back to you by fans?

ANJI: Fans have been great so far. People do write to tell me that my music soothes them. Some have sent me poems, or even love letters. I feel like I've definitely made some friends, through music.

Full interview preserved at lovespirals.com/press/interviews/the-women-of-mp3com/
